Is This Really the FTC? How to Spot a Fake FTC Website or Caller

What this helps with: Spotting fake websites, calls, texts, and emails that say they’re from the FTC (the Federal Trade Commission).

The short answer: A real FTC website always ends in .gov. A real FTC worker will never tell you to move your money to “protect” it, hand over cash or gold, or keep it secret. If someone says that, it’s a scam. Hang up.

The FTC is the U.S. agency that takes scam reports. Scammers know people trust it, so they pretend to be the FTC. In November 2026 the FTC warned about fake FTC websites. It says it has made scammers take down more than 30 of them since April 2024.

How to tell a real FTC website

Look at the end of the web address, at the very top of the screen.

  • Real: It ends in .gov. For example: ftc.gov, consumer.ftc.gov, and ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  • Fake: It ends in something else, like .com, .org, .us, or .online. The FTC says fake sites can copy its name, logo, and look.

Check the address before you type your name, address, or anything about what happened to you. The safest way is to type ReportFraud.ftc.gov yourself. Don’t use a link from a search ad, a text, or an email.

What you’ll tap: The address bar at the very top of your browser. Type reportfraud.ftc.gov and tap Go.

What a real FTC worker will never do

The FTC says a real FTC employee will never:

  • Tell you to move your money to “protect” it. That includes moving it to a “safe” account.
  • Tell you to take out cash or buy gold and give it to someone.
  • Ask you to send money, or pass you to someone else who asks for money.
  • Threaten you, for example to have you arrested or to shut off your phone number.
  • Tell you to keep it secret from your family or your bank.
  • Text you or use a messaging app like WhatsApp to reach you out of the blue.
  • Text you a photo of an ID or badge to “prove” who they are.
  • Offer to get back money you lost to a scam, then ask you to pay or share your bank details.

Also good to know: the FTC doesn’t have “agents” with badge numbers. Caller ID can be faked, so a call that says “FTC” doesn’t prove anything.

What these scams sound like

  • “This is Agent Smith from the FTC. Here’s my badge number.”
  • “Your Social Security number was used in a crime. Move your savings to a safe account today.”
  • “A phone in your name is being used for scams. We’ll shut off your number unless you help.”
  • “I’m with the FTC. I can get back the money you lost, for a small fee.”
  • “Don’t tell your bank. Don’t tell your family.”

The story often starts somewhere else. It might be a pop-up about a virus, or a call from “your bank” or “Amazon.” Then they pass you to a fake FTC worker.

What to do

  1. Hang up, or close the message. You don’t have to explain.
    • What you’ll tap: The red End button on a call.
  2. Don’t send money, cash, or gold. Don’t share a password or a code that was texted to you.
  3. Don’t call back a number they gave you. Don’t tap a link in their message.
  4. Worried about your money? Call your bank. Use the number on the back of your card or on your statement.
  5. Talk to someone you trust before you do anything. Scammers want you to rush. Real help can wait.

Want to reach the real FTC? Type ftc.gov yourself, or call 1-877-FTC-HELP (1-877-382-4357).

If something goes wrong

  • I only answered a call or tapped a link. Close it. Don’t type anything in. You’re probably fine.
  • I typed my information into a fake FTC site. If you gave your Social Security number, go to IdentityTheft.gov for a step-by-step plan. If you gave a password, change it now.
  • I sent money. Call your bank or payment app right away. Ask them to stop or reverse it. The FTC says to act as fast as you can.
  • I let someone into my computer or phone. Turn off Wi-Fi to cut them off. Then follow the steps in My Screen Says I Have a Virus and to Call a Number.
  • Someone offers to get my money back for a fee. That is usually a second scam. Don’t pay. See Someone Says They Can Get Back the Money I Lost to a Scam.
  • It’s not your fault. These scams fool careful people. Telling a family member can help.

Where to report it

  • A fake FTC call, text, email, or website: Tell the real FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. It’s free. Your report can help stop scammers.
  • Lost money online or by phone: You can also tell the FBI at ic3.gov.
  • Free help talking it through: Call the AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline at 877-908-3360. You don’t need to be an AARP member.

Was it a fake bank call, or a request for gold? See Is My Bank Really Calling? and Someone Told Me to Buy Gold to Protect My Money. For more ways to spot a fake, see How to Spot a Scam Call, Text, or Email.

Outside the US?

The reporting services named in this guide are for the United States. If you live elsewhere, report scams to your country’s official service:

  • United Kingdom: Report Fraud (formerly Action Fraud). England, Wales and Northern Ireland. In Scotland, call Police Scotland on 101.
  • Canada: Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre
  • Australia: Scamwatch
  • Ireland: An Garda Síochána: Fraud
  • New Zealand: Netsafe (scams)
  • New Zealand: National Cyber Security Centre (formerly CERT NZ)

In an emergency, call your local emergency number (for example 999 or 112 in the UK and Ireland, 000 in Australia, 111 in New Zealand, or 911 in Canada).
